Delivery baseline for the seven product areas

A first cut at dev time, change failure rate and defect escape rate, measured per product area straight from the C4 architecture model and GitHub. By product area · six months

Each area on its own scale

Every card carries all three segments as figures for the month you pick — use the month buttons, or click any point on any card — and the two that can be drawn as a series are drawn, on one axis. Cycle time is the segment the team owns: first commit in any repository to the last team-branch merge, per ticket — the point at which the work becomes a releasable artifact. Lead time starts at that same first commit and ends when the change is live in production, so it is cycle time plus the wait for a release slot plus the deploy. It is blank wherever no change in the area could be paired to a dated release page, which is most areas in most months — that is why the amber line breaks. Queue time sits between them: the wait from the team branch to main, the release train’s segment. Dating a ticket’s arrival on main needs a merge that names its branch and almost none can be paired: one area-month in the whole window clears the five-ticket floor. So the queue is reported from the other side — how many changes are sitting in it, and how long half of them have been waiting. That is measurable everywhere, and it is the truer number besides: only the fastest tickets can be timed out of a queue, so a median of completed waits is biased short exactly where the problem is. Where a release page’s stated go-live is contradicted by its own changes (June 2026: both main releases slipped and neither page was corrected), the date is re-derived as the last merge plus the median wait of the releases whose dates do hold up, and the card says how many changes rest on one. Both lines are drawn in days on a single shared axis: a second y-scale would let them cross wherever the axis was chosen to make them.

Areas differ by more than an order of magnitude in size, so a shared axis would flatten six of them into a line at the bottom. Each card is scaled to its own maximum instead — every chart starts at zero, the end value is labelled and every point has a tooltip. Compare the shape within a card, not the height between cards.

Read the three figures this way

The three numbers do not add up, and must not be added

Case Administration in August reads cycle 44.1 h, queue age 20.9 d, lead 78.2 d. Those are not three stages of one journey — they are three different populations, measured in three different directions, and only the middle one describes the work you are waiting on:

  • Cycle counts the 64 tickets that reached a team branch during the month. Whether they ever shipped is not part of the figure.
  • Queue age counts the 83 changes that had not reached main when the month ended — by construction the ones that did not get out. Half have been waiting longer than 20.9 days.
  • Lead counts the 14 changes that went live in August, of which 4 carry enough history to date. They are the ones that did get out.

So the same month's cycle, queue and lead describe three disjoint sets of work. A change in the queue-age figure is, by definition, absent from the lead figure. Read each against its own history on the card, not across the row.

Lead time is cycle + queue + deploy — per item, not per median. On every change that shipped and can be traced, the identity holds exactly: first commit → merge, plus merge → go-live, is first commit → go-live, in all 204 of them without exception. What does not hold is the arithmetic on the published figures, for two reasons. Medians do not add: the median dev-and-queue span is 10.6 days and the median deploy wait 9.2, summing to 19.7, while the median of the sums — the real lead time — is 23.3. And the segments above are measured over different populations, so subtracting one from another compares work that shipped against work that did not.

The headline lead time is therefore measured end to end on a single clock, and per ticket, the same unit cycle time uses: from the first commit in any repository the ticket touched to the go-live of the first release that carried it. That gives at the median and at the 85th percentile over tickets — the ones whose release page names a Jira key that can be joined back to the work that started it. It is a small sample and it is the honest one; the per-change figure is kept on the cards, where the ticket join is too thin to cut seven ways.

One correction this page has already made. Lead time read 9.4 days here until the origin clock was checked. A team branch is squashed into a single commit on the way to main, so that pull request's oldest commit is the merge itself: 209 of the 413 changes that shipped in the window date their own merge and nothing earlier, and a lead time taken from them is the release wait wearing a longer name. Lead time is now measured only over the 204 whose history survives, and every card says how many of its released changes those are. The platform median moved from 11.0 days to 23.3.

A correction to the unit

One feature, branched in every repository it touches

A CPP feature is not one pull request. It is cut as a branch in every repository it needs, so one piece of work arrives as several changes. Measured per pull request, a feature that took a fortnight across four services reported as four short changes — and every cycle-time figure in earlier versions of this page was that number. Changes are now folded into tickets by the Jira keys already in their titles and branches.

Where the time actually sits

Three segments, three different owners

Merging to a team branch is not the end of the journey; it is the start of a wait. Work merges to the team branch when it is done, then waits for the current release’s testing to finish, is rebased and retested, and only then merges to main. So main sits close to production, and the queue sits between the team branch and main.

Measured

Why the split is the point, and not an accounting nicety

A correction. An earlier version of this panel said the release train was capped at 20 tickets and was over capacity in every month of the year. The release pages do not support that. Counting Jira keys on the 20 scheduled releases that went live between March and August, scope ran at a mean of 29 tickets and a median of 28, from 14 to 58, and 13 of the 20 carried more than 20. Whatever the intended batch size, the releases that shipped were not held to it. About 98 distinct tickets go live per month across roughly three to four scheduled releases plus patches — the cadence is not reliably weekly either.

What stands independently of any of that, because it is measured from git rather than from a stated policy, is the backlog: changes merged in these six months are not on main, and only of tickets going via a team branch have every repository landed. Work is accumulating before main, and that is why the segments are reported apart — a blended end-to-end figure would charge the teams for a wait they do not control. But the size of the release batch is not established as its cause, and this page should not be read as saying it is.

One further correction this exposed: of tickets that go through a team branch, only have every repository on main. The 13–23% reported elsewhere on this page is a per-change figure carried almost entirely by merges that go straight to trunk, which are on main the moment they merge. The two are not comparable and should not be quoted together.

Measured

Not every change takes the same route

The team branch is the route, and of changes take it. The rest are not feature branches jumping the queue — that is not permitted — and they are not the team-branch and release-branch hops either, which are excluded as integration before any of this is counted. They are two much narrower things:

Repositories with no integration branch. Six trunk-based services never cut one, so their merge into main is hop one. Nothing is skipped; the two-hop model simply does not apply to them, and their cycle time is directly comparable to everyone else’s.

Exceptions inside repositories that do use team branches. This is the residue, and it is not yet fully explained. Of these, 80 carry a work-item key that also merged through a team branch in the same repository, which has the shape of a release fix being forward-ported onto main — the same change counted twice, once on each route. 128 carry a key seen only on the direct merge, and 56 carry no key at all. Branch names here (dev/rel-3rd-line-2613-main, dev/main-ali-2615) suggest release work rather than new features, but the names alone cannot settle it.

Everything with an unambiguous plumbing name — copy-…, tmp/…, merge-…, …-main-merge, rel26… — is now excluded as integration, as are merges into a develop integration branch, which is why the totals here are slightly below earlier revisions. The exception bucket is a known open question, not a finding: until it is resolved, treat the blended median as carrying a small, unquantified double-count.

The second hop

How much of this work is actually on main?

Cycle time stops at the merge into the team branch, which is where roughly four changes in five go. So it answers “how long to be accepted”, not “how long to reach trunk”. This asks the second question. Whether a change is on main is settled per change — by asking GitHub whether its own merge commit is contained in main — so nothing here depends on a branch being named tidily.

Why the time-to-main median is a floor

This is censored data. The median can only be computed over changes that reached main, and those skew heavily towards changes that went straight there — which is why it sometimes reads lower than cycle time. On its own it flatters. Read it with the share on main beside it, the same way the median needs the 85th percentile.

The migration · its own lane

Two delivery models, measured apart

Repositories are moving to Modern by Default: trunk-based development on short-lived feature branches, with a GitOps route to production. Those repositories have no integration branch, so their merge into main is the first hop. Blending them with the rest would time a shorter journey for one group and the full journey for the other, in the same column. Each repository’s lane is read from its own merges over the year, not from a list, so a repository moves lane when its branching does.

The finding

The gap is arrival, not speed

Over six months the trunk-based repositories put 100% of their changes on trunk, every single month, with nothing left in flight. The integration-branch repositories ran at 13–23%, leaving changes sitting on branches trunk does not have.

That is the strongest signal in this dataset, and it is the one the headline cycle-time number cannot show.

Do not compare these

Cycle time is not the measure here

On cycle time the trunk-based lane often reads slower. That is an artefact: their clock runs all the way to trunk, while the other lane’s stops at the integration branch with the second hop still ahead of it. Compare the two lanes on time to trunk and the share arriving, never on cycle time.

Not measurable yet

We cannot see the GitOps route

Lead time is measured backwards from the release manager’s pages, which record the weekly release train. Of 380 changes traced to a production date, 1 came from the trunk-based lane — GitOps deploys outside the train and leaves no release page behind.

So the 11.1-day lead time is a release-train figure. It says nothing about Modern by Default, and the part these services would most improve — the nine-day post-merge wait — is exactly the part invisible for them. Measuring it needs Flux or AKS deployment events, not release pages.

The third clock · measured backwards

How long until it reaches users?

The two clocks above start at a commit and ask how far it got. This one starts at a release and works backwards. Every weekly release has a Tech Focused page in Confluence carrying a go-live date and the list of pull requests that shipped in it; joining those back to their first commit gives lead time end to end, against a real production date rather than a proxy. Measured against a real go-live date

Nine days of the eleven are spent after the code is finished

Building a change takes a median of a few hours. Getting it in front of a user takes another nine days. That split is stable in every month we can measure, and it is the single most useful thing in this report: the constraint is not how fast people write code.

A weekly release train puts a floor of about 3.5 days under that wait on average. The observed wait is roughly nine. So the train is part of the answer, but only part — the rest is the queue between merging and being picked up by a release.

Survivorship — read this before quoting the number

Every change here is one that made it

A change only appears on a release page if it shipped. Work still sitting on a team branch never appears, and the table two sections up says only 16–30% of merged changes reach main at all. So eleven days is how long it takes when it works, not how long work takes. It is a lower bound.

Read it beside the share on main, never instead of it. The two together bracket the truth: hours to merge, eleven days to ship if it ships, and most things not shipping in the window at all. The gap between those is the queue.

Two limits in the source

Why June is missing

The go-live date on a release page is the planned date, and when a release slips the page is not always corrected. Three June releases list changes that merged after their own stated go-live — for one of them, all 21 of them. Those releases are dropped rather than measured against a date that would produce a negative wait, which is why June shows one change against the highest merge count of any month. That is a gap in the source, not a collapse in delivery, and it is worth fixing at the page.

Separately, about one pull-request link in six on the release pages does not resolve — 89 of 495 return a 404 from deleted branches, renamed repositories or mistyped numbers. Those changes are simply invisible here.

Method

How each number is made

The repository-to-area mapping is generated from the LikeC4 model in cp-c4-architecture, so it stays correct as the model changes and there is no second list to maintain. 75 repositories map to 25 products across the seven areas. An area is a subdomain of the model, with no per-product exceptions: cpp-ui-home was previously promoted to an area of its own and now reports inside Platform Engineering, where the model puts it.

Read this first

Why the median, not the average

The distribution is not merely skewed. Across 2,119 changes in six months, half merge within about 2.7 hours of their first commit, while one in twenty takes over a month and those few carry 74% of the total elapsed time. Averaging the same changes gives 5.8 days. Both are arithmetically correct: the median says how long a typical change takes, an average says how much branch age is sitting in the repositories. If you have seen a figure near 30 days, it is an average — usually an average of per-repository averages, which lets one dormant repository weigh as much as a busy one. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions.

Measured

Cycle time

Hours from the first commit on a feature branch to that change’s merge into its team branch — the first hop — reported as median and 85th percentile. It stops at that merge, so release cadence and deployment lag are invisible to it. Four changes in five take that route; the rest are services with no team branch, plus an unexplained residue, both reported apart above.

Proxy

Change failure rate

Corrective changes divided by all changes, where “corrective” means the title, branch or labels match revert, rollback, hotfix, incident or production fix. It counts changes that were fixes, not deployments that failed. Connecting Azure DevOps replaces it with failed main-branch pipeline runs.

Not available

Defect escape rate

Defects found in production over all defects found. Every repository has GitHub Issues effectively unused, so the tool reports nothing rather than a misleading 0%. Jira credentials and an agreed definition of “found in production” would turn this on.

Excluded

What is filtered out

The flow is feature branch → team branch → main → cut a release branch for SIT, and a change is counted once, on its first hop — the merge where the work landed. Kept out of all three metrics: automated and housekeeping changes (Renovate, Dependabot, version bumps); the second hop, a team/* or release branch merging into main, whose features were already counted and whose clock is the branch’s age; and main being rebased back into a team branch. 474 of 2,593 changes over six months, 18%. A fix made on a release branch is still counted, where it was made.

What we need from you

Eight open questions

Each of these is a decision currently made by a default in the script. Every one of them changes the numbers above. Answers, disagreements and “that is not how we work” are all useful.

Unit

Is the product area the right unit to report on?

Case Administration covers 25 repositories and 118 changes a month; Common Platform User Interface covers one repository and six. Putting them in the same table invites a comparison that is not meaningful. We can report at product level instead (25 rows), or keep areas and simply suppress rates below a volume threshold.

Scope

Which of the three clocks is the headline number?

We can now measure all three: hours to merge, days-to-weeks to reach main, and about eleven days to production for work that ships. Cycle time flatters, because it stops at a team branch. Lead time flatters differently, because it only counts winners. Our view is that the honest headline is a pair — lead time next to the share that reaches main — but a single number is what usually gets asked for. Which pair, or which single number, do you want to be held to?

Definition

Should we report a “shipped within 30 days” rate instead of a duration?

Most of the difficulty above comes from trying to express a censored, heavily skewed distribution as one duration. A completion rate — what share of changes started in a month were live within 30 days — sidesteps the censoring entirely, cannot be gamed by picking easy work, and is legible without a footnote. It is a different question from “how long”, and it may be the more useful one.

Source

Can the release pages be trusted as a measurement source?

They are the only record that ties a production date to specific pull requests, and they are maintained by hand. Three June releases carry stale go-live dates and about one PR link in six is dead. None of that matters for running a release; all of it matters for measuring one. Either the pages become a data source we hold to a standard, or lead time needs a second source — the pipeline repository, or Azure DevOps deployment records — to check them against.

Failure

What should count as a failed change?

The current proxy reads titles and branch names for revert, hotfix, incident and production fix. It will miss any failure that was rolled forward quietly, and it will count a fix that never reached production. If Azure DevOps pipeline outcomes are the agreed definition, we need an organisation, project and a read-only PAT.

Defects

Which Jira field tells us a defect escaped?

The script can already infer each area’s Jira projects from the work-item keys in their change titles — CHD and RV for Court Hearing, SPRDT and LPT for Scheduling & Listing, PDE for MI. What it cannot guess is how you record that a defect was found in production: a label, an environment field, an issue type, or a specific board.

Signal

Is August’s jump real?

Change failure rate went from a steady 1.6–2.0% across five months to 6.0% in August, driven by 21 corrective changes against a normal 4–8. That is either a real quality signal worth investigating, or a change in how branches and titles are named. Someone close to the work needs to look before this is read as a trend.

Use

What is this for?

These measures behave very differently as a team’s own improvement signal than as a cross-team comparison in a delivery report. The second use tends to change behaviour rather than measure it. Worth agreeing the intent before the dashboard gets a wider audience.
